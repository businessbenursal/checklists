    Check 1

    First impression and visual credibility

    Would a homeowner trust this company to walk into their house, based on the site alone?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    People judge how a site looks almost instantly. In a 2006 study by Gitte Lindgaard and colleagues at Carleton University, participants rated web pages after seeing them for just 50 milliseconds, and those ratings closely matched the ratings they gave after looking longer. That first impression tends to color how people judge everything that follows.

    Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) lists design quality as one of four factors people use to judge whether a business is trustworthy, alongside being upfront with information, having complete and current content, and showing up on the rest of the web (reviews, social). For services that come into the home, NN/g found people want to see the actual work and the people doing it, not just finished rooms.

    Got it right

    In an NN/g usability study, 4 of 5 participants comparing two snack-box services chose BoxGreen over GuiltFree, saying its pictures, fonts, and colors were more appealing. NN/g notes that when prices and offerings are similar, the website’s design becomes a major deciding factor.

    Evidence type: small qualitative usability study (2016). A comparison, not a before/after test.

    Got it wrong

    In the same study, participants noticed UniHomeCleaning only showed photos of clean rooms, not the cleaning process or the cleaners, and they spent time on competitors’ photos showing real cleaners in homes. Separately, one participant’s trust in a moving company dropped after spotting a single typo on its homepage.

    Evidence type: usability-study observations (NN/g, 2016).
    The business test for “it looks old.” Never recommend a redesign just because a site looks dated. Only raise visual design when at least one of these is true: it breaks or is hard to use on a phone; it shows no real proof of their work; it has visible errors or stale content; or clutter hides the contact info and estimate button. Then frame it as trust and estimate requests, not style. There’s no reliable public study showing a painting company’s redesign alone increased leads, so don’t promise one.

    How to fix it

    • Show real proof. Their own project photos, before/after pairs, and crews at work. Real photos beat stock, especially for a business that enters people’s homes.
    • Keep the visual system simple. A small, consistent color palette that fits the trade, one or two fonts, and enough white space. NN/g notes white space makes content feel well organized.
    • Fix credibility killers first. Typos, broken links and images, expired promos, old years. These are quick wins.
    • Make it work on a phone. Readable without zooming, buttons easy to tap, nothing cut off or overlapping.
    • Put contact info where people look. Phone number in the header, tappable on mobile.
    Say it in the video“Homeowners are deciding whether to let a crew into their house, and they make that call fast. Right now the site shows stock photos, so there’s no proof of your work up top. Swapping in your real before/after shots gives them that proof in the first few seconds.”
    Check 2

    Headline and hero message

    In 10 seconds, can a homeowner tell what they do, where, and what to do next?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    A Microsoft Research study of page-visit data (Liu, White, and Dumais, 2010), summarized by NN/g, found that the first 10 seconds are critical: visitors are most likely to leave early, and those who stay past about 30 seconds tend to stay much longer. NN/g’s takeaway is that you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds.

    Accuracy note: you’ll often see “the average visitor stays 10–20 seconds” attributed to NN/g. That’s a misquote. The finding is about how critical the first 10 seconds are, not an average visit length.

    Got it right

    37signals tested five headline and subheadline combinations on the Highrise signup page. The winner, “30-day Free Trial on All Accounts” with “Sign-up takes less than 60 seconds” underneath, produced 30% more signups than the original. It led with what the customer gets and removed the sense of effort.

    Evidence type: company-reported A/B test (Signal v. Noise blog, 2009). Software signup page, not a painter.

    Got it wrong

    The original headline, “Start a Highrise Account,” was the worst performer of the five. It named what the company wanted the visitor to do instead of what the visitor would get, the same mistake as a painter’s headline that just says “Welcome to ABC Painting.”

    Evidence type: same company-reported test.

    How to fix it

    Headline formula:

    [Service] in [area] + [outcome or differentiator]

    Example: “Interior and exterior painting for Tampa homes. On time, spotless cleanup, free written estimates.”

    • The headline must have: what they do, where they do it, and why choose them. Plain words a homeowner would search for.
    • Avoid: the company name as the headline, “Welcome,” and slogans that could fit any business (“Quality you can trust”).
    • Subheadline reduces risk: free estimates, licensed and insured, a warranty. Only claims that are true for them.
    • One primary button above the fold, worded the same as the form button (“Get my free estimate”).
    • Tappable phone number in the header on mobile.
    • A trust strip near the top: Google rating and review count, years in business. Real numbers only.
    • Hero image: one of their real finished projects.

    SEO: the H1 and H2s

    The visible headline should also be the page’s main heading in the code (the H1), written as real text rather than baked into an image or slider. Google’s John Mueller has said headings help Google understand what each part of a page is about. He has also said there’s no special ranking boost from an H1 and that Google handles pages with several H1s fine. So treat headings as clarity for people and Google, not a ranking trick.

    How to check: right-click the headline and choose Inspect, or use a free headings-outline browser extension.

    • H1 = the hero headline: service plus area, in real text. “Interior and Exterior Painting in Tampa.” One clear H1 per page is good practice.
    • H2s for the main sections: each service, “Our process,” “Recent projects,” “Reviews,” “Areas we serve.”
    • The subheadline is a paragraph, not another heading.
    • Don’t use headings for styling or stuff keywords into them (“Tampa painter painting Tampa painters”).
    • Common painter-site problems: the H1 is the logo or “Welcome,” the headline lives inside a slider image, or every section is an H1.
    Say it in the video“When someone lands here, the first thing they read is ‘Welcome to [Company].’ It doesn’t tell them you paint homes in [City] or what to do next. A headline that says exactly that, with an estimate button right under it, answers their questions in the first few seconds.”
    Check 3

    Estimate request form and button

    Once a homeowner is ready, how easy is it to ask for an estimate?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Don’t submit test entries on a prospect’s live form. Judge it by looking.

    Why it matters

    Every field adds effort. Baymard Institute’s research on e-commerce checkouts found most sites need only 8 form fields, yet the average checkout in 2024 had 11.3. In their testing, 42% of participants typed their full name into the “First Name” field at least once when names were split; with a single “Full Name” field, only 4% hesitated. The context is online stores, but the principle carries over to any form.

    NN/g saw the same frustration with service quotes. A participant on a moving company’s site said they just wanted a quick quote and hated keying in all the details. NN/g’s conclusion: the effort was too high, and it’s fine to trade some precision for a simpler form.

    Got it wrong

    Expedia had an optional “Company” field on its booking form. Some customers entered their bank’s name, then the bank’s address, so credit card verification failed. After deleting that one field, Expedia reported about $12 million a year in added profit.

    Evidence type: company-reported figure (Expedia VP Joe Megibow, 2010, reported by ZDNet/Silicon.com).

    Got it right

    On an Unbounce landing page, changing the button from “Start your free 30 day trial” to “Start my free 30 day trial” increased click-through by 90% over a three-week test. CRO consultant Michael Aagaard reported similar first-person wins on other sites.

    Evidence type: practitioner A/B test on one page. Results vary by site, so present it as an idea worth testing, not a guarantee.

    How to fix it

    • Fields: full name, phone, email, zip code, service (dropdown). Optional: notes and photo upload. Move everything else to the follow-up call.
    • Button text: an action plus what they get, in first person. “Get my free estimate.” Never “Submit.”
    • Below the button: what happens next and how fast (“We’ll call or text within one business day”), only if it’s true for them.
    • Labels: clear labels above each field; mark optional fields as optional.
    • Placement: on the homepage near the top and on every service page, not just the contact page. On mobile, a sticky “Get estimate” button and tap-to-call.
    • Consistency: the hero button, the form heading, and the form button all use the same words.
    Say it in the video“When someone’s ready, the form asks for [number] things and ends with a ‘Submit’ button. Cutting it to name, phone, email, zip, and service, with a button that says ‘Get my free estimate,’ makes saying yes a lot easier.”
    Check 4

    Page speed on mobile

    Does the page show up fast on a phone, or do people stare at a blank screen?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Test at pagespeed.web.dev on mobile. If it shows real-user data, trust that over the lab score. Lab results vary, so run it 2–3 times.

    Why it matters

    Google’s Core Web Vitals measure how fast the main content appears (Largest Contentful Paint), how quickly the page responds to taps (Interaction to Next Paint), and how much it jumps around (Cumulative Layout Shift). Google’s “good” thresholds are about 2.5 seconds, 200 milliseconds, and 0.1.

    In a study commissioned by Google and run by 55 and Deloitte across 37 large brands, a 0.1-second improvement across four speed metrics was associated with an 8.3% better bounce rate on lead-generation information pages and 8.4% more conversions on retail sites. These are big brands, not painters, but the direction is consistent.

    Got it right

    Vodafone A/B tested a landing page. The optimized version showed its main content in 5.7 seconds instead of 8.3 (31% faster) and produced 8% more sales and a 15% better lead-to-visit rate.

    Evidence type: company A/B test, published on Google’s web.dev (2021).

    Got it wrong

    The other side of the same test: Vodafone’s original, slower page (8.3 seconds) was its own control, and it brought in fewer sales and leads than the faster version with the same offer.

    Evidence type: same company A/B test.
    Speed is one of many signals in Google’s ranking systems, and relevant content still matters more. Don’t promise rankings from speed alone; frame it as keeping visitors who would otherwise leave.

    How to fix it

    • Resize and compress images, and use modern formats like WebP or AVIF. Hero photos from a phone camera are often several megabytes.
    • Replace sliders and autoplay video at the top with one optimized image of their work.
    • Set image width and height so the layout doesn’t jump while loading.
    • Delay non-essential scripts like chat widgets and extra trackers; remove unused WordPress plugins.
    • Use decent hosting with caching and a CDN where it makes sense.
    • No popups on load on mobile.
    Say it in the video“On a phone, your main image takes about [X] seconds to show up, and a lot of people give up before that. Compressing the images and replacing the slider would get your work on screen much faster.”
    Check 5

    Service pages and service area

    Can a homeowner find a page about the exact job they need, in their area?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    Each main service is its own search (“cabinet painting near me”) and its own decision. A dedicated page can answer that job’s questions with prep, process, timeline, and photos of that kind of work, and it gives Google a clear page to match to the search.

    NN/g found people judge whether a company really serves them by whether the site shows that kind of work. Homeowners were put off by moving companies that seemed to cater mostly to commercial moves, even though they also did residential ones.

    Got it right

    In NN/g’s study, participants spoke favorably of moving companies that offered thorough, useful content like packing tips. NN/g found that complete, relevant content signals expertise and commitment to customers.

    Evidence type: usability-study observations (NN/g, 2016).

    Got it wrong

    Google’s spam policies name “multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page” as doorway abuse, and treat blocks of city names on a page as keyword stuffing. Copy-paste city pages can hurt rather than help.

    Evidence type: official Google policy (Search Central spam policies).
    There’s no reliable public before/after study for painter service pages, so frame this as helping homeowners and Google find the right page, not a guaranteed ranking jump.

    How to fix it

    • One page per core service, each with: a headline (service plus area), what’s included, prep and process, typical timeline, real photos of that service, FAQs (price factors, paint brands, prep), related reviews, and an estimate form.
    • A service area page: the areas they cover, ideally a map, and real local projects.
    • City pages only with real local content like projects and reviews from that city. Never just swap the city name.
    • List each service in the menu and link related services to each other.
    Say it in the video“Right now everything is on one Services page, so someone looking for cabinet painting has to dig. A dedicated cabinet page with your cabinet photos and process gives them, and Google, a clear match.”
    Check 6

    Google search listing

    When they show up on Google, does the listing say what they do and where?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    How to check: search Google for their company name, and for site:theirdomain.com to see each page’s listing. Google can rewrite listings, so also check the title in the page source.

    Why it matters

    The title link and snippet are what searchers read before deciding whether to click. Google says it builds title links mainly from the page’s title element (it may also use headings and other text) and sometimes uses the meta description for the snippet when it describes the page better than the page text does.

    Got it right

    Google’s guidance: give every page its own title, make it descriptive and concise, and brand it concisely. On the home page, the title can include a short description alongside the name.

    Evidence type: official Google documentation (“Influencing your title links”).

    Got it wrong

    Google specifically tells site owners to avoid vague descriptors like “Home” for the home page, to avoid repeated boilerplate titles across pages, and to avoid keyword stuffing.

    Evidence type: official Google documentation.
    There’s no reliable public case study isolating title changes for a local service business, so present this as a clarity fix that helps people choose them in search, not a guaranteed traffic jump.

    How to fix it

    [Service] in [City] | [Company]
    • Homepage: “House Painters in Tampa, FL | Sunshine Painting.” Service page: “Cabinet Painting in Tampa | Sunshine Painting.”
    • Unique title for every page, service and city first, short enough not to get cut off. Google has no official length limit.
    • Meta description: one or two sentences on what, where, proof, and next step. “Interior and exterior painting for Tampa homes. 180+ Google reviews, licensed and insured. Get a free written estimate.” Real numbers only.
    • Expect Google to rewrite sometimes. That’s normal; clear titles and descriptions still give it the best material.
    Say it in the video“When I search for [Company], the listing just says ‘Home,’ so someone comparing painters can’t tell what you do. A title like ‘House Painters in [City] | [Company]’ tells them right away.”
    Check 7

    Reviews and proof

    Does the site show real proof that past customers were happy?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Check their Google Business Profile too: if they have strong reviews the site doesn’t show, that’s an easy win to point out.

    Why it matters

    Hiring a painter is a high-consideration purchase. Northwestern University’s Spiegel Research Center, working with PowerReviews, analyzed online retail data and found purchase likelihood for a product with five reviews was 270% higher than for one with none. Reviews mattered more for higher-priced items, and purchase likelihood peaked at ratings of 4.0–4.7 rather than a perfect 5.0.

    NN/g found every participant in its service-business study said they would read reviews before hiring, and they trusted external review sites more than testimonials on the company’s own site.

    Got it right

    In the Spiegel data, as products began displaying reviews, conversion rates rose quickly, with most of the gain coming from the first handful of reviews.

    Evidence type: large data analysis of unnamed online retailers (2017), not home services.

    Got it wrong

    Faking it is now costly. The FTC’s rule on reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024, bans fake reviews (including AI-written ones), buying reviews, and undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties of up to $51,744 per violation (2024 figure).

    Evidence type: US federal regulation (FTC).

    How to fix it

    • A rating strip near the top: Google rating, review count, and a link to the Google profile.
    • 3–6 real reviews with first name and last initial, neighborhood or city, the service, and ideally a photo of that job.
    • Pair reviews with project photos so proof and praise sit together.
    • Ask every customer after the job, with a text containing a direct link to leave a Google review.
    • Never write, edit, buy, or fake reviews, and back up any claim like “#1” or “most trusted.”
    Say it in the video“You have [number] Google reviews at [rating] stars, but the homepage doesn’t show them. Putting that rating near the top, with a few real reviews next to your job photos, puts your best proof where people decide.”
    Check 8

    Navigation and mobile usability

    Can people find services, past work, and contact info without hunting?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    In a quantitative NN/g study with 179 participants, people on desktop used hidden menus (like hamburger icons) in only 27% of cases, versus 48–50% for visible or partly visible menus. NN/g reported that hiding navigation cut discoverability almost in half, made tasks take longer, and made them feel harder.

    NN/g also found that plain, meaningful menu labels signal that a company understands what customers need, while vague or clever labels leave people unsure whether the content even exists.

    Got it right

    Sites in the study with visible or combined navigation saw people use the menu about half the time and find content more easily.

    Evidence type: quantitative usability study (NN/g, 2016) on six news and e-commerce sites.

    Got it wrong

    Desktop sites that hid their menu behind an icon saw it used in only 27% of cases, and participants were slower and rated tasks as harder.

    Evidence type: same NN/g study.

    How to fix it

    • On desktop, show the main menu with plain labels: Services (with each service listed), Our Work, Reviews, Areas We Serve, About, Contact.
    • Add a “Get my free estimate” button in the header on every page.
    • On mobile, a menu icon is fine, but keep Call and Estimate visible in the header. Labeling the icon “Menu” helps.
    • Make tap targets large and spaced out; Google’s web.dev guidance suggests about 48×48 pixels.
    • Fix dead or mislabeled links.
    Say it in the video“On a computer, your menu is hidden behind an icon, so people don’t see right away that you do [services]. Putting the main links across the top, with an estimate button, shows everything at a glance.”
    Check 9

    Value proposition clarity

    At a glance, can a homeowner say what they do, who it’s for, and why pick them?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    A value proposition is the specific reason a homeowner should choose this painter. NN/g’s summary of Microsoft Research data found the first 10 seconds decide whether people stay, and recommends communicating your value proposition within that window (see Check 2).

    Evidence

    Pages that survive the first 10 seconds tend to keep visitors much longer; past about 30 seconds, people often stay 2 minutes or more. A clear reason to stay is what gets them past that point.

    Evidence type: large-scale browsing research (Liu, White, and Dumais, 2010), summarized by NN/g.

    Evidence gap

    There’s no reliable public before/after study on value propositions for painting contractors. Present this as making their best reason visible, not as a guaranteed lift.

    Honest note.

    How to fix it

    For [who] in [area] who want [outcome], we [what we do]. Unlike most painters, we [differentiator].
    • Use the formula internally, then turn it into a headline, subheadline, and 3 short benefit lines under the hero.
    • Lead with the customer’s outcome; move company history to the About page.
    • Keep the same core message on every page.
    Say it in the video“After 5 seconds on your homepage, I could tell you paint houses, but not why someone should pick you over the other painters in [City]. Your reviews keep mentioning [X]. Putting that front and center gives people a reason.”
    Check 10

    Differentiation

    Would this site still make sense with a competitor’s logo on it?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    When competitors look the same, people fall back on surface cues. In NN/g’s comparison study (Check 1), when prices and offerings were similar, the website’s design became a major deciding factor. A real, specific differentiator gives homeowners a reason based on substance instead.

    Evidence

    NN/g’s observation that look-alike offerings push people toward surface cues supports making a real difference visible.

    Evidence type: indirect, from a small usability study (NN/g, 2016).

    Evidence gap

    No reliable public study measures generic slogans versus specific claims for local contractors. This check is a strategy principle and is labeled as one.

    Honest note.

    How to fix it

    • Mine their reviews for what customers praise most. That’s usually the real differentiator.
    • Pick 1–3: a specialty (cabinets, historic homes), a process (prep, protection, daily cleanup), a promise (warranty length, start-date commitment), people (owner on site), or a number (homes painted).
    • Put it in the hero subheadline and a “Why homeowners choose us” section, with proof under each point.
    • Only claims they can back up.
    Say it in the video“Your reviews keep mentioning [X], but the site says ‘quality work at fair prices,’ which every painter says. Leading with [X] gives people a real reason to choose you.”
    Check 11

    Benefits over features

    Does the copy say what the homeowner gets, not just what the company does?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    Homeowners aren’t buying paint; they’re buying the result and an easy experience. NN/g’s research shows people read only a fraction of a page’s words, so the benefit has to be stated outright rather than left for them to work out. In Highrise’s headline test (Check 2), the winning version led with what the customer gets and how little effort it takes.

    Evidence

    Highrise’s winning subheadline, “Sign-up takes less than 60 seconds,” turned a feature into a benefit (low effort) and was part of the combination that produced 30% more signups.

    Evidence type: company-reported A/B test (2009). Software, not a painter.

    Evidence gap

    No reliable public study compares benefit-led and feature-led copy for painting contractors. Frame this as clearer copy, not a guaranteed lift.

    Honest note.

    How to fix it

    [Benefit], because [feature as proof]
    • Lead with the benefit, then give the feature as proof: “Live normally during the job. We cover floors and furniture and clean up every day.”
    • Run the “so what?” test on every claim until it reaches something the homeowner feels.
    • Keep benefits truthful and specific. Avoid promises the company can’t back up.
    Say it in the video“Your page says you use premium paint, which is great, but it doesn’t say what that means for the homeowner. Spelling out the benefit, like [benefit], turns a feature into a reason to hire you.”
    Check 12

    Ideal customer fit

    Does the site attract the jobs they actually want?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    NN/g found people judge whether a company serves “people like me” from what the site shows. Homeowners were put off by movers that seemed to focus on commercial moves, and cleaning services that only showed high-end homes or big offices turned away people in smaller homes. As NN/g put it, no one wants to feel like a second-rate customer.

    Got it right

    Participants spent time on and preferred service sites whose photos and content matched their own situation, such as real cleaners working in homes like theirs.

    Evidence type: usability-study observations (NN/g, 2016).

    Got it wrong

    HomeCleanz’s portfolio page showed only large condo complexes and office buildings, so participants questioned how often it actually worked with smaller homes.

    Evidence type: usability-study observation (NN/g, 2016).

    How to fix it

    • Decide the primary customer and make the first screen and gallery reflect them.
    • Give each audience a path if they serve several (“For homeowners,” “For HOAs and property managers”).
    • Match proof to the audience: photos and reviews from the kind of customer they want more of.
    • Feature the services they want to grow near the top.
    Say it in the video“Your gallery is mostly [commercial] jobs, so a homeowner might wonder if you do houses like theirs. Leading with [residential] projects shows them they’re exactly your kind of customer.”
    Check 13

    Risk reversal and guarantees

    Does the site make hiring them feel safe?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    Hiring a painter feels risky: strangers in the house, money paid up front, a mess, a result they’ll live with for years. NN/g found people value sites that are upfront about anything that affects the customer experience, including links to guarantees, and that sites leaving out basic information were quickly ruled out.

    Evidence

    NN/g lists presenting guarantees among the upfront details that create a feeling of transparency and show a company understands its customers.

    Evidence type: usability study (NN/g, 2016).

    Evidence gap

    No reliable public study isolates warranty messaging for painting contractors, so don’t promise a specific lift.

    Honest note.

    How to fix it

    • State specific terms (“2-year workmanship warranty”) and link to the details. Only what the company actually offers.
    • Put “Licensed and insured” near the top, with the license number where relevant.
    • Make a protection promise: floors and furniture covered, daily cleanup.
    • Keep guarantees honest and clear. US law (the FTC’s guides on advertising warranties) expects advertised guarantees to be real and their terms clear.
    Say it in the video“You mention a warranty only on the About page. Putting ‘[warranty], licensed and insured’ right under your headline answers the biggest worry before people have to ask.”
    Check 14

    Offer and reason to act

    Is there a clear, honest reason to reach out now?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    “Contact us” asks for commitment without giving anything. A low-risk first step lowers the bar. Fake urgency is the wrong way to do it: the FTC’s 2022 staff report on dark patterns lists countdown timers on offers that aren’t really time-limited, and false scarcity claims, as design tricks that create false beliefs.

    Evidence

    Highrise’s headline test (Check 2) found leading with a free, low-risk first step (“30-day Free Trial”) produced 30% more signups than a plain “Start an account.”

    Evidence type: company-reported A/B test (2009). Software, not a painter.

    Got it wrong

    The FTC names baseless countdown timers and false “almost gone” claims as deceptive design patterns that it watches for enforcement.

    Evidence type: FTC staff report, “Bringing Dark Patterns to Light” (2022).

    How to fix it

    • Offer an easy first step: a free written estimate, a quote from photos, or a free color consult, but only what they truly offer.
    • Use real timing only: “Exterior season books up by April” if it’s true for them.
    • Remove fake timers and scarcity claims.
    Say it in the video“Right now the only option is ‘Contact us.’ Offering an easy first step, like [offer], makes reaching out feel low-risk.”
    Check 15

    Speed to lead

    Does the site promise a fast reply and offer quick ways to reach them?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    In a 2011 Harvard Business Review study, researchers sent a test inquiry to 2,241 US companies: 37% responded within an hour, 23% never responded, and the average reply among those who did respond within 30 days took 42 hours. A companion analysis of about 1.25 million leads found companies that tried to reach a lead within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify it as those that waited even one more hour, and more than 60 times as likely as those that waited 24 hours or longer.

    Got it right

    Firms that followed up within the first hour were far more likely to have a real conversation with the decision maker.

    Evidence type: large research study, Oldroyd, McElheran, and Elkington, HBR (2011). Mixed B2B and B2C companies, not painters.

    Got it wrong

    Nearly a quarter of the audited companies never replied to the inquiry at all.

    Evidence type: same HBR study.
    This is mostly about their operations, not just the site, which makes it a strong opening for you: form alerts to their phone, an instant confirmation text, and a stated reply time are exactly the kind of automation you build.

    How to fix it

    • State a real reply time near the form and button.
    • Add a text option: “Text us photos for a fast quote.”
    • Send an instant confirmation by email or text with what happens next.
    • Route new inquiries to the owner’s phone so nothing waits in an inbox.
    • Show business hours near the contact options.
    Say it in the video“Your form doesn’t say when people will hear back. Harvard Business Review research found companies that respond within an hour are far more likely to actually reach the customer. A text option and an instant ‘we got it’ message help you win those first-hour leads.”
    Check 16

    Pricing guidance

    Does the site give any sense of cost or what affects it?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    NN/g watched people rule out service businesses that hid basic information. One participant spent only 35 seconds on a cleaning site before deciding against it because it didn’t state rates. Another, on a moving company’s site, said they just wanted a quick quote and hated keying in all the details. NN/g concluded it’s fine to give ranges as long as the relevant costs are clear.

    Got it right

    NN/g recommends sacrificing some precision for ranges, as long as the main cost factors are shown, rather than forcing a long quote form.

    Evidence type: usability study (NN/g, 2016).

    Check 39

    Video

    Is there a short video that shows the people and the work?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    A short video can show the owner’s face, the crew, and how a job runs, which supports the trust needs in Checks 36 and 37. Be careful with statistics here: most widely quoted numbers about video and conversions come from video companies’ own surveys.

    Evidence

    NN/g’s finding that people want to see the process and the people doing in-home work supports using video to show both.

    Evidence type: indirect, from NN/g usability research (2016).

    Evidence gap

    There’s no reliable independent study showing video increases leads for painters. Vendor surveys don’t count, so don’t quote them.

    Honest note.
    A natural place to mention your own video skills, but keep it low-key: one sentence, as an option.

    How to fix it

    • A 30–60 second owner intro on the homepage or About page.
    • Short project clips: before, process, after.
    • Click to play, no autoplay with sound, and a lightweight thumbnail so the page stays fast.
    • Reuse their social videos on the site.
    Say it in the video“You’ve got great project videos on Instagram, but none on your website. A short clip of you introducing the crew, right on the homepage, lets homeowners meet you before you knock on their door.”
    Check 40

    Conversion tracking

    Can the owner tell which leads came from the website?

    Spot it

    0 problems found

    Why it matters

    Without tracking, the owner can’t tell which pages, ads, or channels produce estimate requests, so marketing decisions are guesses. Google Analytics 4 can record form submissions and calls from the site as key events, and Tag Manager is a common way to set that up.

    Evidence

    Google’s GA4 documentation describes marking important actions, like form submissions, as key events so their sources can be measured.

    Evidence type: official Google Analytics documentation.

    Evidence gap

    This is practical rather than research-based, and from outside you can only see whether tags exist, not whether they’re set up correctly. Phrase it as “it looks like,” and ask.

    Honest note.
    A strong pitch angle that doesn’t criticize their design at all: “Right now you can’t tell which jobs came from your website.” It connects directly to Check 15 and your automation work.

    How to fix it

    • Install GA4 (directly or through Tag Manager).
    • Track key events: form submissions, phone taps, text taps.
    • Connect ad platforms so conversions report back.
    • Add a thank-you page or clear confirmation after the form.
    • Send a simple monthly summary of leads by source to the owner.
    Say it in the video“From what I can see, your site isn’t tracking form or call leads, so it’s hard to know which of your marketing actually brings in jobs. Setting that up means you’d see exactly where each estimate request comes from.”
