    Framing vs manipulation
    
  
    Framing is choosing how to present true information. Manipulation is hiding, inventing, or pressuring.
    
  
    
    
    Framing (do this)
    Choosing which true facts to lead with, giving a price the right comparison, helping a prospect calculate their own numbers, recommending an option and explaining why.
    
    
    Manipulation (never this)
    Inventing losses or urgency, fake “regular prices,” promising results you can’t control, hiding limits, pressuring someone who said no.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Three quick tests
    
    
      
      
    • The notes test: would I be comfortable if the client read my notes on how I framed this?
      • 
      
    • The numbers test: is every number true, and ideally theirs?
      • 
      
    • The fairness test: would this still feel fair to them if they knew the technique?
      • 
    
    
    
    US rules that apply: the FTC’s advertising substantiation policy (claims need a reasonable basis), Guides Against Deceptive Pricing (no fake reference prices), guides on warranties and guarantees, the 2022 dark patterns report (no fake urgency), and the 2024 reviews and testimonials rule (no fake reviews).
    
  
    

    


    
  
    Lesson 1Offer
    Frame the cost of waiting
    Help prospects see what “not now” actually costs, in their own numbers.
    
  
    The principle
    Most prospects don’t say no to you; they say “not now.” Your job is to help them see what “not now” actually costs, honestly and in their own numbers, so they make a real decision instead of a default one.
    
  
    Why it works
    People feel losses more strongly than equal gains. In a classic 1981 study, Tversky and Kahneman showed that people make different choices about the same outcome depending on whether it’s described as something gained or something lost. Later research found loss framing can prompt more action, though the effect is often modest and depends on the situation.
    The practical takeaway: “You’ll get more clients” is easy to postpone. “You’re losing clients every month this stays the same” is harder to ignore, as long as it’s true.
    Research: Tversky and Kahneman (1981); Meyerowitz and Chaiken (1987). Caveat: meta-analyses show the effect is often small, so being specific matters more than the frame itself.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Make the cost concrete, not dramatic.
      Vague losses sound like sales talk. Specific ones sound like advice.
      Weak“Your website is costing you business.”
      Stronger“Your contact form is three clicks deep. Anyone ready to hire you has to hunt for it.”
    2. Let them calculate it.
      A number you claim invites doubt. A number they work out becomes their own conclusion. Ask instead of asserting.
      Say“About how many inquiries do you get in a month?”
      Say“What’s a typical client worth to you over a year?”
      Say“If one more inquiry a month became a client, what would that be worth?”
    3. Pair the cost with a small, safe next step.
      Pointing out a loss without an easy way forward just creates anxiety. Give them something low-risk to say yes to.
      Say“We don’t have to do everything. Fixing just the form would stop the biggest leak.”
    4. Say so when waiting is fine.
      If the cost of waiting is genuinely small, tell them. It costs you one deal and earns trust, referrals, and a prospect who comes back when the timing is right.
      Say“Honestly, your site is doing its job. I’d revisit this next year rather than now.”
    5. Stay calm.
      Urgency should come from the facts, not your tone. A calm description of a real cost is more persuasive than excitement.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “This looks good, but let’s talk again next quarter.”
    You: “That’s completely fine. Before we pause, can I ask two quick questions so you can decide with real numbers?”Asking permission keeps it respectful.
    Prospect: “Sure.”
    You: “Roughly how many inquiries come through the site each month, and what’s a new client usually worth to you?”They supply the numbers.
    Prospect: “Maybe 10 inquiries. A client’s worth about $2,000.”
    You: “So if the changes turned even one more of those 10 into a client each month, that’s about $2,000 a month, or $6,000 by next quarter. If that feels small, waiting makes sense. If not, we could start with just the form fix now.”A cost they calculated, an honest way out, and a small next step.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Inflating the numbers. If they catch one exaggeration, they’ll doubt everything else.
    • Fear without a way out. It feels like pressure, and pressure makes people back away.
    • Fake urgency, like “this price ends Friday” when it doesn’t. The FTC treats false urgency as deceptive.
    • Using it on every prospect. If waiting is truly fine for them, say so.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write the two questions you’d ask a prospect to help them calculate their own cost of waiting. Then say the example conversation out loud using your offer.
    Show an example answer
    1. “About how many inquiries come through your site in a typical month?”
2. “Roughly what’s a new client worth to you over a year?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 2Offer
    Reframe price as value over time
    A price is always judged against something. Choose an honest comparison before the prospect chooses a bad one.
    
  
    The principle
    A price is never high or low on its own; people judge it against something. If you don’t give your price a comparison, the prospect will pick one for you, usually the cheapest quote they found.
    
  
    Why it works
    Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that a starting number, or anchor, strongly shapes later judgments. Gourville (1998) found that describing a cost as a small daily amount made people more willing to pay, because it changed what they compared it to: small everyday expenses instead of a big purchase.
    The practical takeaway: you can’t stop prospects from comparing, but you can offer a fairer comparison, such as the outcome, the time span, or the real cost of the cheaper option.
    Research: Tversky and Kahneman (1974); Gourville (1998). Framework: “compared to what?” is standard in consultative selling.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Never state a price alone.
      A bare number gets compared to the cheapest number they’ve seen. Attach what it includes and what it’s for.
      Weak“It’s $3,000.”
      Stronger“It’s $3,000 for the full rebuild, including copy, tracking, and three months of fixes.”
    2. Compare to the outcome, not the competitor.
      The most meaningful comparison is what the work is worth to them.
      Stronger“You said a client is worth about $2,000. This pays for itself with two new clients.”
    3. Break it down when the cost is ongoing.
      For monthly or recurring work, a smaller unit can be clearer, as long as it’s accurate and not a trick.
      Stronger“$400 a month works out to about $13 a day to keep the site bringing in work.”
    4. Move from hourly rate to total cost.
      Hourly rates are easy to compare and misleading. What matters is total hours, quality, and rework.
      Stronger“The rate matters less than how many hours it takes and whether it has to be redone.”
    5. Hold the price; change the scope.
      If their budget is real, remove work rather than discounting. Discounts teach clients your price was padded.
      Stronger“I can’t do the full rebuild for $2,000, but I can do the top three fixes for that.”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “I found someone on a freelance site for $15 an hour.”
    You: “That’s a big difference, and it’s worth comparing properly. Did they estimate how many hours it would take?”No defensiveness; move to total cost.
    Prospect: “Not really.”
    You: “That’s usually where the real cost hides. I’ve done this kind of work for 15 years, so I can give you a fixed scope and stand behind it. If their estimate comes out to the same scope for less, it may be the better choice, and I’d tell you that.”An honest comparison, plus a fair way out.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Discounting the moment someone says “too expensive.”
    • Criticizing the cheaper competitor. It sounds defensive.
    • Inventing a “regular price” to make a discount look bigger. The FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing cover that.
    • Dodging the price question. Evasion feels like hiding something.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your main price three ways: the total, per week or month, and next to the cost of the alternative.
    Show an example answer
    Total: “$400 for a 10-hour block.”
Per week: “About $30 a week over a quarter, and unused hours never expire.”
Vs the alternative: “Less than redoing a cheap fix that breaks, or losing one client.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 3Offer
    Sell the outcome, not the deliverable
    Clients don’t buy websites; they buy what the website does for their business.
    
  
    The principle
    Describe the result in the client’s business first and your work second. Tasks invite comparison on hourly rate; outcomes invite comparison on value.
    
  
    Why it works
    Marketing professor Theodore Levitt is credited with the line that people don’t want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. Clayton Christensen later built this into jobs-to-be-done theory: customers “hire” a product or service to make progress in their lives or businesses.
    When you describe tasks (“WordPress development”), a prospect can only compare you on price. When you describe outcomes (“more booked calls”), they compare you on value, which is where your experience matters.
    Framework: Levitt; Christensen, Competing Against Luck (2016). Evidence gap: no controlled study shows outcome-led descriptions win more service deals; use it because it’s clearer for buyers.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Lead with the result, follow with the method.
      Put the outcome first and the how second, so they know why the work matters before hearing what it is.
      Weak“I’ll rebuild your homepage in Pro.”
      Stronger“The goal is more estimate requests. Rebuilding the homepage is how we get there.”
    2. Use “so that” to turn tasks into outcomes.
      Every deliverable should finish the sentence “so that…”.
      Stronger“Speed improvements, so visitors don’t leave before the page loads.”
      Stronger“Tracking setup, so you know which marketing brings in clients.”
    3. Use the client’s measures, not yours.
      They measure calls, jobs, and hours saved, not page speed scores or plugin counts.
      Weak“Your Lighthouse score will improve.”
      Stronger“More people will stay long enough to call you.”
    4. Keep outcomes believable.
      Promise the direction and how you’ll measure it, not numbers you can’t control.
      Weak“I’ll triple your leads.”
      Stronger“We’ll track inquiries from day one, so you’ll see whether it’s working.”
    5. Save technical depth for proof.
      Your technical skill is why the outcome is likely, so bring it in when they ask how, not as the headline.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “So what exactly do you do?”
    You: “I help service businesses turn more of their website visitors into booked calls.”Outcome first.
    Prospect: “How?”
    You: “Usually it’s a few things: making it obvious what you do and how to reach you, simplifying the contact form, and setting up tracking so we can see what’s working. I’ve built WordPress sites for 15 years, so the technical side is the easy part.”Method second; experience as proof.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Leading with jargon and tools.
    • Promising outcomes you can’t influence, like rankings or specific revenue.
    • Vague outcomes (“grow your business”) that sound like every other freelancer.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    List five things you deliver, then rewrite each one with “so that…”.
    Show an example answer
    “Contact form setup, so every inquiry reaches you within minutes.”
“Speed optimization, so visitors don’t leave before the page loads.”
“Tracking setup, so you know which marketing brings in clients.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 4Offer
    Risk reversal
    Make saying yes feel safe by promising what you control.
    
  
    The principle
    Before someone hires you, they’re weighing what happens if it goes wrong. Reduce that risk with specific promises about what you control, never about results you don’t.
    
  
    Why it works
    NN/g’s trust research found people value companies that are upfront about guarantees, and that this transparency builds trust. Hiring a freelancer, especially one overseas, adds perceived risk: will they disappear, go over budget, or deliver something unusable?
    Guarantees on things you control (timeline, revisions, communication, file ownership) directly answer those fears. The FTC expects any advertised guarantee to be real and its terms clear.
    Research: NN/g, Trustworthiness in Web Design (2016). Regulation: FTC guides on warranties and guarantees. Evidence gap: no reliable study measures guarantees for freelance services specifically.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Name the risk they’re probably feeling.
      Saying the fear out loud shows you understand it and lowers their guard.
      Stronger“Most people worry about paying for work that drags on or never quite gets finished.”
    2. Guarantee the process, not the results.
      Timeline, revisions, response time, and ownership are yours to control. Rankings and sales are not.
      Weak“Guaranteed first page on Google.”
      Stronger“You’ll have a first draft in 7 days, and you own every file.”
    3. Make it specific and in writing.
      Vague guarantees sound like slogans. Specific ones sound like commitments.
      Weak“Satisfaction guaranteed.”
      Stronger“If the first draft isn’t on track, I’ll revise it until it is, at no extra cost.”
    4. Use structure to lower risk.
      How you sell can reduce risk too: small first projects, milestone payments, or prepaid hours that never expire.
      Stronger“You can start with a 10-hour block. Hours never expire, so there’s no risk of paying for work you don’t need.”
    5. Only promise what you’ll honor every time.
      One broken guarantee costs more than never offering one.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “How do I know this will actually work?”
    You: “Fair question. Honestly, no one can promise a number of leads, and I’d be careful with anyone who does.”Honesty builds credibility.
    You: “What I can promise: a first draft in 7 days, revisions until it matches what we agreed, and tracking from day one so you can see the results yourself. And you start with a small block of hours, so you’re never locked in.”Specific, controllable, low-commitment.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Guaranteeing rankings, leads, or revenue.
    • Vague guarantees with no terms.
    • Guarantees with hidden conditions that make them meaningless.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write two guarantees based only on what you control.
    Show an example answer
    1. “You’ll have a first draft within 7 days, or the next 5 hours are free.”
2. “You own every file and login from day one.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 5Offer
    Name and package your offer
    A clear, named offer with fixed scope beats “I do websites, hourly.”
    
  
    The principle
    A named offer with a defined scope, timeline, and price is easier to understand, compare, recommend, and say yes to than an open-ended hourly service.
    
  
    Why it works
    Packaging reduces uncertainty. The buyer knows exactly what they get, when, and for how much, and it’s easier for them to repeat to a partner or refer to a friend. Practitioners call this a productized service.
    You may have heard that fewer choices always sell better, based on the famous 2000 “jam study.” A 2010 meta-analysis found the average effect of choice overload was close to zero, so the real reason to keep packages few is clarity, not a magic number.
    Framework: productized services (practitioner method). Research, mixed: Iyengar and Lepper (2000); Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, and Todd (2010).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Name it after the outcome.
      The name should tell them what it does for them.
      Weak“Website Package B.”
      Stronger“The Lead-Ready Website.”
    2. Define includes, excludes, timeline, and price.
      Clear edges prevent scope creep and surprises on both sides.
      StrongerIncludes: 5 pages, copy, form alerts, tracking. Excludes: ads, photography. Timeline: 3 weeks. Price: $3,000 fixed.
    3. Keep hourly for small, open-ended work.
      Packages fit defined projects; hours fit ongoing fixes and improvements.
      Stronger“For a full build, use the package. For small changes after that, use a block of hours.”
    4. Make it repeatable.
      The same process every time makes you faster, more consistent, and easier to explain.
    5. Avoid hype names.
      A name that overpromises sets up disappointment and sounds like marketing.
      Weak“The Guaranteed Growth Engine.”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Can you just do it hourly?”
    You: “Yes, hourly works well for small changes. For a full build, the package is usually better for you, because the price is fixed. You know exactly what you’ll pay, and if it takes me longer, that’s on me.”Frames the package as protecting them.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Too many packages, so choosing becomes work.
    • Vague inclusions that cause arguments later.
    • Renaming the same thing to look bigger.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write the package card for your main offer: name, includes, excludes, timeline, and price.
    Show an example answer
    Name: The Lead-Ready Website
Includes: 5 pages, copy, form + alerts, tracking
Excludes: ads management, photography
Timeline: 3 weeks
Price: $3,000 fixed
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 6Offer
    Good, better, best
    Real options turn “yes or no?” into “which one?”
    
  
    The principle
    One price invites a yes-or-no decision. Three genuine options let clients choose their level of investment, and change the question from “should I?” to “which one?”
    
  
    Why it works
    Huber, Payne, and Puto (1982) showed that adding a clearly weaker option can increase the choice of the option it resembles, known as the decoy effect. Simonson (1989) found people often favor the middle option when choices trade off price and features, known as the compromise effect.
    These effects vary and aren’t guaranteed. The honest reason to offer options is that clients genuinely have different budgets and needs, and options let them choose for themselves.
    Research: Huber, Payne, and Puto (1982); Simonson (1989). Caveat: effects vary by situation.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Build each option around a different need.
      Each tier should solve a real version of the problem, not just add padding.
      StrongerQuick-Win: stop the biggest leaks. Lead-Ready: rebuild for inquiries. Growth: keep improving monthly.
    2. Describe the differences in their terms.
      Explain what each one does for them, not just what’s added.
      Weak“Tier 2 adds 3 pages and GA4.”
      Stronger“Lead-Ready rebuilds the site around getting inquiries, and shows you where they come from.”
    3. Recommend one, and say why.
      Clients appreciate guidance. A recommendation with a reason is helpful, not pushy.
      Stronger“Based on what you told me, I’d recommend Lead-Ready, because your main problem is the site itself.”
    4. Make the smallest option genuinely useful.
      Budget-limited clients should still get real value, which often opens the door to more work later.
    5. Never include a fake decoy.
      Every option must be one you’d gladly deliver.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “That’s more than I planned to spend.”
    You: “That’s fine. There are three ways to do this. The Quick-Win option fixes the three biggest issues for $1,200. The full rebuild is $3,000. If budget is the concern, I’d start with Quick-Win and do the rest when it makes sense.”Options, an honest recommendation, and a path forward.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Options too similar to tell apart.
    • More than three or four choices.
    • A decoy option you’d never want to sell.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Design three tiers for your main offer, with what each one does for the client.
    Show an example answer
    Quick-Win ($1,200): fix the top 3 issues.
Lead-Ready ($3,000): rebuild around inquiries, with tracking.
Growth ($3,000 + $400/month): rebuild plus 10 hours a month of improvements.
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 7Offer
    Show the full scope before the price
    The order you present information changes how the price feels.
    
  
    The principle
    Lead with their goal, the cost of the current situation, and the work. Then state the price. The first number a prospect hears becomes their reference point, so make it a real one.
    
  
    Why it works
    Tversky and Kahneman (1974) showed that starting numbers anchor later judgments. Ariely, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2003) found even arbitrary numbers influenced how much people were willing to pay.
    If the first number they hear is a competitor’s low quote, everything after it looks expensive. If the first thing they consider is what the problem is costing them and everything the work includes, your price is judged against real value.
    Research: Tversky and Kahneman (1974); Ariely, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2003). Regulation: FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Open with their goal and the cost of the current situation.
      This sets the real reference point before any price appears.
      Stronger“Right now, by your estimate, the site loses about two inquiries a month.”
    2. Walk through the scope in their terms.
      Show everything included, so they see the full value before the number.
    3. Then state the price plainly, once.
      No hedging, no apology. Pause after you say it.
      Stronger“The investment is $3,000.”
    4. If asked early, give an honest range.
      Refusing to give any number feels evasive. A real range, plus what moves it, is honest and keeps the conversation going.
      Stronger“Projects like this usually land between $2,500 and $4,000, depending on two things. Can I ask about those?”
    5. Never anchor with a fake price.
      A made-up “regular price” is deceptive. Anchor with real scope and real costs.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Just give me a number.”
    You: “Sure. Projects like this usually land between $2,500 and $4,000. Where you fall depends on how many pages and whether I write the copy. Can I ask about those two things?”An honest range, then back to scope.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Leading with the price in an email or proposal.
    • Guessing low to keep them interested, then raising it later.
    • Refusing to give any price, which feels like hiding.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Reorder this proposal outline: Price, Timeline, Your goals, What’s included, About me.
    Show an example answer
    1. Your goals (and what the current situation costs)
2. What’s included
3. Timeline
4. About me
5. Price
    

    


    
  
    Lesson 8Value proposition
    Specific beats impressive
    Concrete details are more believable than big adjectives.
    
  
    The principle
    Replace impressive-sounding words with specific facts. “Award-winning, top-quality service” is forgettable and easy to doubt; “sites live in 3 weeks, 15 years building WordPress” is memorable and checkable.
    
  
    Why it works
    In Morkes and Nielsen’s 1997 study, rewriting promotional web copy in a plain, objective style improved measured usability by 27%. Promotional claims made people stop and question them, which distracted from the message.
    Precise numbers also carry weight. Janiszewski and Uy (2008) found that precise numbers act as stronger anchors than round ones, because people assume someone who gives a precise figure has a reason for it. That only helps if the number is true.
    Research: Morkes and Nielsen (1997); Janiszewski and Uy, Psychological Science (2008). Regulation: the FTC expects objective claims to have a reasonable basis.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Swap adjectives for facts.
      Every adjective should be replaceable with a fact that proves it.
      Weak“Experienced, reliable developer.”
      Stronger“Building WordPress sites since 2014; coding since 2008.”
    2. Use real numbers, not rounded hype.
      A true, specific number is more believable than a big round one.
      Weak“Hundreds of happy clients.”
      Stronger“Led a team that built client sites for 4 years.”
    3. Name concrete details.
      Names, timelines, and tools make claims tangible.
      Stronger“First draft in 7 days. You own every file and login.”
    4. Show, don’t claim.
      A screenshot, link, or short example proves more than any adjective.
      Stronger“Here’s the form I rebuilt for an appliance store, and how leads now reach the owner by text.”
    5. Never stretch a fact.
      Specific claims are easy to check. One exaggeration undoes all the rest.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Why should I go with you?”
    You: “Three reasons, specifically. I’ve built WordPress sites since 2014, so the technical side won’t slow us down. You’ll have a first draft in 7 days. And you’ll get tracking from day one, so you can see exactly what’s working.”Three checkable facts instead of adjectives.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Stacking adjectives (“premium, innovative, world-class”).
    • Round, inflated numbers.
    • Specific claims you can’t back up when asked.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Rewrite this line using only specific facts: “I’m an experienced, reliable, high-quality web developer.”
    Show an example answer
    “I’ve built WordPress sites since 2014, led a custom-build team at Themeco for four years, and deliver first drafts within 7 days.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 9Value proposition
    Position against the real alternative
    You’re not compared with nothing. Know what they’d do instead, and frame yourself against that.
    
  
    The principle
    Every prospect has an alternative: another freelancer, an agency, a DIY builder, their nephew, or doing nothing. Your value only makes sense next to that alternative, so name it and show the difference honestly.
    
  
    Why it works
    April Dunford’s positioning framework starts with competitive alternatives: what customers would do if you didn’t exist. Often the strongest competitor is doing nothing or doing it themselves.
    NN/g found that when offerings look similar, people fall back on surface cues. If you don’t make the difference clear, prospects decide on price or looks alone.
    Framework: April Dunford, Obviously Awesome (2019). Research: NN/g (2016), indirect.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Find out what they’d do otherwise.
      Ask early, so you know what you’re really being compared with.
      Say“If we don’t work together, what would you do instead?”
    2. Position against each alternative differently.
      The case against an agency isn’t the case against DIY.
      StrongerVs agency: “You work directly with the person doing the work, at a fraction of the overhead.”
      StrongerVs DIY: “You get your evenings back, and it’s built to bring in work, not just look nice.”
      StrongerVs doing nothing: “The current site keeps costing you inquiries each month.”
    3. Acknowledge what the alternative does well.
      Fair comparisons are more believable than attacks.
      Stronger“A DIY builder is great for getting online cheaply. Where it falls short is turning visitors into calls.”
    4. Lead with the difference that matters to them.
      Pick the one difference that solves their stated problem.
    5. Never disparage competitors.
      Criticizing others makes you look insecure. Compare facts, not people.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Honestly, I was thinking of just using a website builder myself.”
    You: “That’s a reasonable option, and it’s cheaper. Can I ask what you want the site to do for you?”Acknowledge, then ask.
    Prospect: “Mainly get more people to call.”
    You: “Then the question is whether you want to spend your evenings learning what makes people call, or have someone who already knows set it up. If you enjoy that kind of thing, DIY can work. If not, that’s where I come in.”Positions against DIY on their goal and time.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Assuming your competitor is another freelancer when it’s really “do nothing.”
    • Attacking competitors.
    • Using one generic pitch for every alternative.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    List three alternatives your prospects consider, and write one honest line positioning you against each.
    Show an example answer
    Agency: “You work directly with the person building your site, without agency overhead.”
DIY: “You get your time back, and it’s built around getting calls.”
Do nothing: “The current site keeps costing you inquiries every month it stays the same.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 10Value proposition
    Borrowed credibility, used accurately
    Your background, employers, and tools can build trust, if you describe your role exactly.
    
  
    The principle
    Credentials, past employers, and associations transfer trust, but only when described precisely. Overstating your role is the fastest way to lose credibility when someone checks.
    
  
    Why it works
    Robert Cialdini’s work on influence identifies authority as one of the main reasons people say yes: we rely on credible experts. Credentials and recognizable names act as shortcuts for trust.
    Those shortcuts only work if they hold up. The FTC’s Endorsement Guides prohibit implying an endorsement or relationship that doesn’t exist, and LinkedIn makes most claims easy to check.
    Research summary and framework: Cialdini, Influence (1984, revised 2021). Regulation: FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR 255).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Describe your role exactly.
      Say what you did, not what the company did.
      Weak“I helped build the Pro page builder.”
      Stronger“I led Themeco’s Elite team, which built custom client sites using Pro.”
    2. Connect the credential to their problem.
      A credential only matters if it explains why you can help them.
      Stronger“I’ve used Pro since my Themeco days, so I can fix things most developers have to research first.”
    3. Use years and specifics, not titles alone.
      “15 years” and named work say more than “senior” or “expert.”
    4. Never imply endorsement.
      Mentioning a past employer is fine; suggesting they recommend you is not, unless they do.
      Weak“Themeco-approved developer.”
      Stronger“Former Themeco team lead.”
    5. Let proof follow the claim.
      Offer a link, portfolio piece, or reference right after.
      Stronger“Happy to send links to sites I built, and say exactly what I did on each.”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Have you worked with Cornerstone before?”
    You: “Yes, quite a lot. I worked at Themeco, the company behind it, from 2017 to 2021, where I led the Elite team that built client sites with Pro. I still build with it today.”Precise role, dates, and relevance.
    Prospect: “So you built the builder?”
    You: “No, that was the product team. My side was building sites for clients with it, which is closer to what you need.”Correcting the overstatement builds more trust than letting it stand.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Letting a prospect believe a bigger role than you had.
    • Name-dropping without connecting it to their problem.
    • Using logos of companies you didn’t work with directly.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write two sentences describing your strongest credential exactly, and how it helps a client.
    Show an example answer
    “I led Themeco’s Elite team from 2017 to 2021, building custom client sites with the Pro page builder. That means I can solve Pro problems quickly that most developers would have to research.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 11Value proposition
    Turn gaps into honest strengths
    Address your weaknesses before they do, and show the real upside.
    
  
    The principle
    Every seller has gaps: no track record in an industry, working solo, a different time zone. Raising a gap yourself, and honestly showing what comes with it, is more persuasive than hoping no one notices.
    
  
    Why it works
    O’Keefe’s 1999 meta-analysis of persuasion research found that two-sided messages which acknowledge a counterargument and then answer it are more persuasive than one-sided messages. Two-sided messages that raise a weakness without answering it were less persuasive.
    So the rule is: name the gap, then answer it with something true. Don’t hide it, and don’t just confess it.
    Research: O’Keefe, meta-analysis of one-sided vs two-sided messages, Communication Yearbook (1999).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Raise it before they do.
      Bringing it up yourself shows confidence and removes the awkward moment.
      Stronger“You might be wondering about time zones, since I’m in the Philippines.”
    2. Answer it with a real upside.
      Not spin: a genuine benefit that comes with the situation.
      StrongerSolo: “You work directly with me. No hand-offs, no account managers.”
      StrongerTime zone: “I can work while you sleep, so changes are often ready by morning, and I’m available during your business hours for calls.”
    3. For no track record, offer a fair trade.
      If you lack results in their industry, offer reduced risk or a lower price in exchange for a case study.
      Stronger“You’d be one of my first clients in this industry, so I’m offering a reduced rate in exchange for sharing the results.”
    4. Don’t over-apologize.
      Name it once, answer it, move on.
      Weak“Sorry, I know I’m just one person and far away and…”
    5. Never turn a gap into a false claim.
      “No track record” can’t become “proven results.”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Have you done this for companies like mine?”
    You: “Not in your industry yet, and I want to be upfront about that. What I have done is build lead-focused sites for home services companies, which works the same way.”Name the gap, then the closest true proof.
    You: “Because you’d be one of my first in this space, I’d set up tracking before changing anything, so we both see exactly what improves. And the rate reflects that.”A fair trade: measurement and price.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Hiding the gap and hoping it doesn’t come up.
    • Confessing it without answering it.
    • Spinning it into a claim that isn’t true.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Pick your biggest gap. Write one line naming it and one line with an honest upside or fair trade.
    Show an example answer
    Gap: “I don’t have case studies with measured lead growth yet.”
Upside: “So I set up tracking before changing anything, and price the first projects lower in exchange for sharing results.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 12Value proposition
    Social proof that fits
    Proof from people like the prospect beats generic praise.
    
  
    The principle
    People are most persuaded by what others like them did. One relevant testimonial from a similar business beats ten generic ones.
    
  
    Why it works
    Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius (2008) found hotel guests reused towels more when told most guests had done so, and even more when told guests who stayed in the same room had. The closer the match to the person’s situation, the stronger the effect.
    Spiegel Research Center found reviews strongly increase purchase likelihood, especially for higher-priced decisions. And the FTC’s 2024 rule bans fake or misleading reviews and testimonials.
    Research: Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius, Journal of Consumer Research (2008); Spiegel Research Center (2017). Regulation: FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials rule (2024).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Match proof to the prospect.
      Show testimonials from the same industry, size, or problem when you have them.
      StrongerFor a contractor: “Here’s what an appliance store owner said about getting inquiries by text.”
    2. Make testimonials specific.
      Name, business, and a concrete result or detail.
      Weak“Great work! — J.”
      Stronger“Leads now reach my phone within minutes. — Store owner, Texas.” (with permission)
    3. Use proof at the moment of doubt.
      Place it right where the prospect hesitates: next to pricing, or after a claim.
    4. Say how many, when it’s true.
      Real numbers of clients or projects add weight.
    5. Never fake or embellish.
      Edited, invented, or paid-for testimonials are banned under the FTC’s rule.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Has this worked for anyone else?”
    You: “The closest example is an appliance store. Their inquiries were landing in an inbox no one checked, so I set up a text alert to the owner’s phone. The owner said leads now reach him within minutes.”Similar situation, specific detail, accurate.
    You: “I can put you in touch with them if you’d like.”A reference is stronger than a quote.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Generic praise that could apply to anyone.
    • Proof from a very different kind of client.
    • Rewording testimonials so they say more than the client did.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Pick your best piece of proof and rewrite it so it’s specific and matched to your target client.
    Show an example answer
    “For an appliance store, I connected their website form to a text alert, so new inquiries reach the owner’s phone within minutes.” (Use only what’s true and permitted.)
    

    


    
  
    Lesson 13Sales conversations
    Questions before pitches
    Let the prospect name the problem before you offer the solution.
    
  
    The principle
    The person who asks the questions guides the conversation. When prospects describe their own problem, they believe it more, and you learn exactly what to offer.
    
  
    Why it works
    Neil Rackham’s research on about 35,000 sales calls, published as SPIN Selling (1988), found that successful salespeople in larger sales asked more questions, especially about problems and their consequences, instead of pitching early.
    Harvard researchers Huang and colleagues (2017) found that people who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were liked more by their conversation partners. Sales-call analytics firm Gong has also reported that top performers in its data talked less than half the time.
    Research: Rackham, SPIN Selling (1988); Huang et al., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2017). Practitioner data: Gong Labs analysis of recorded sales calls.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Start with their situation, then the problem.
      Open broad, then narrow.
      Say“Walk me through how new clients usually find you.”
      Say“Where does that tend to break down?”
    2. Ask about consequences.
      The cost of a problem is what creates motivation.
      Say“What happens when an inquiry slips through?”
      Say“How much is that costing you, roughly?”
    3. Ask follow-up questions.
      Follow-ups show you’re listening and uncover the real issue.
      Weak“Okay. So, about my services…”
      Stronger“You said leads go to an old inbox. How often do you check it?”
    4. Let them say what a fix would mean.
      When they describe the benefit, it becomes their goal, not your pitch.
      Say“If that were fixed, what would change for you?”
    5. Talk less than half the time.
      Your job in discovery is to understand, not impress.
    
  
    Example in action
    You: “Before I suggest anything, can I ask how new clients usually find you?”Permission, then a broad question.
    Prospect: “Mostly referrals, and some from the website.”
    You: “And when someone comes through the website, what happens next?”Follow-up.
    Prospect: “They fill out the form… and honestly, sometimes we don’t see it for days.”
    You: “What does that usually mean for the lead?”Consequence.
    Prospect: “We’ve probably lost a few to faster companies.”They named the problem and its cost themselves.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Pitching in the first five minutes.
    • Asking questions just to set up a pitch, then not listening to the answers.
    • Interrogating: rapid-fire questions with no follow-ups.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write five discovery questions for your main offer: two about their situation, two about problems and consequences, one about what a fix would mean.
    Show an example answer
    Situation: “How do new clients usually find you?” / “What happens after someone fills out the form?”
Problem: “Where does that break down?” / “What does a missed inquiry cost you?”
Fix: “If that were solved, what would change for you?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 14Sales conversations
    Label what they’re feeling
    Naming an emotion out loud calms it and shows you understand.
    
  
    The principle
    When a prospect is worried, frustrated, or hesitant, say what you notice: “It sounds like…” Naming the feeling lowers its intensity and makes them feel understood, which opens the conversation back up.
    
  
    Why it works
    Lieberman and colleagues (2007) found that putting feelings into words reduced activity in the brain’s emotional alarm center (the amygdala). They called this affect labeling.
    Former FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss built this into a sales and negotiation method in Never Split the Difference (2016): calm, tentative labels like “It seems like…” rather than “I understand.”
    Research: Lieberman et al., Psychological Science (2007). Framework: Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference (2016).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Use tentative openers.
      Start with “It sounds like,” “It seems like,” or “It looks like,” not “I understand” or “You feel.”
      Weak“I understand how you feel.”
      Stronger“It sounds like you’ve been burned by a web person before.”
    2. Label, then pause.
      Let them respond. Silence after a label often brings out the real concern.
    3. Label the negative before it grows.
      Naming a likely worry early takes its power away.
      Stronger“It probably seems like one more person promising more leads.”
    4. Don’t follow a label with “but.”
      “But” cancels the label and turns it into a setup.
      Weak“It sounds frustrating, but my process is different.”
      Stronger“It sounds frustrating.” (pause)
    5. Only label what you genuinely notice.
      Labels used as a trick sound fake and backfire.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “The last guy we hired took our money and disappeared halfway through.”
    You: “It sounds like you’re worried the same thing could happen again.”A label, no defense.
    Prospect: “Exactly. I don’t want to go through that twice.”
    You: “That makes sense. Would it help if we started with a small block of hours, so you can see how I work before committing to more?”Address the feeling with a concrete, low-risk step.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Saying “I understand” instead of naming what you see.
    • Labeling and immediately pitching.
    • Over-labeling until it sounds like therapy.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write a label for each: (1) “We’ve been burned before.” (2) “This is a lot of money for us.” (3) “I’m not very technical.”
    Show an example answer
    1. “It sounds like trust is the big question here.”
2. “It seems like you want to be sure this pays off before you commit.”
3. “It sounds like you want someone who’ll explain things without jargon.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 15Sales conversations
    Summarize to agree
    Repeat their situation back so well they say “that’s right.”
    
  
    The principle
    Before proposing anything, summarize what you heard in their words. When a prospect says “that’s right,” they feel understood, and your proposal becomes a response to their problem rather than your pitch.
    
  
    Why it works
    Research on active listening, such as Weger and colleagues (2010), found that paraphrasing what someone said made them feel more understood.
    Chris Voss argues that the goal is to hear “that’s right,” which signals real agreement, rather than “you’re right,” which is often a polite way to end the discussion.
    Research: Weger, Castle, and Emmett, International Journal of Listening (2010). Framework: Chris Voss (2016).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Use their words, not yours.
      Repeat their phrases so they recognize their own situation.
      Stronger“So leads are going to an inbox no one checks, and you think you’ve lost a few to faster companies.”
    2. Include the cost and the goal.
      A good summary covers the problem, what it costs, and what they want.
      Stronger“…and what you want is every inquiry reaching you within minutes.”
    3. Ask if you got it right.
      Invite correction; it makes the summary more accurate and more theirs.
      Say“Did I get that right, or did I miss something?”
    4. Then propose, tied to the summary.
      Link each part of your proposal to what they said.
      Stronger“Since speed is the issue, the first thing I’d fix is the alert system.”
    5. Keep it short.
      Three or four points, not a recap of the whole call.
    
  
    Example in action
    You: “Let me make sure I’ve got this. Leads come through the form, but they go to an inbox nobody checks often, so some take days to answer. You think you’ve lost a few jobs to faster companies, and what you want is to hear about every inquiry right away. Is that right?”Their words, cost, and goal.
    Prospect: “That’s exactly it.”
    You: “Then here’s what I’d suggest, starting with the alerts…”The proposal follows directly from their agreement.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Summarizing in your own jargon.
    • Skipping the summary and going straight to your pitch.
    • Summarizing what you wanted them to say instead of what they said.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Think of a recent prospect conversation. Write a three-sentence summary: the problem, its cost, and their goal, ending with a question.
    Show an example answer
    “So your site gets visitors but few inquiries, and the ones that come in sit in an old inbox. You think that’s costing you a couple of jobs a month. What you want is more inquiries that reach you fast. Did I get that right?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 16Sales conversations
    Always frame the next step
    End every conversation with a specific, small, scheduled next step.
    
  
    The principle
    Good conversations die without a next step. Before you hang up, agree on exactly what happens next, who does it, and when.
    
  
    Why it works
    Research on implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999) found that people who decide when and where they’ll act follow through much more often than those who only intend to.
    In a field study, Milkman and colleagues (2011) found that employees prompted to write down the date and time they’d get a flu shot were more likely to get one. Specific plans beat good intentions.
    Research: Gollwitzer, American Psychologist (1999); Milkman et al., PNAS (2011).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Make it specific.
      Name the action, the day, and the time.
      Weak“Let me know what you think.”
      Stronger“I’ll send the proposal Thursday. Can we talk Friday at 10 your time to go over it?”
    2. Make it small.
      A small next step is easier to say yes to than a big decision.
      Stronger“Can you send me access to your contact form settings so I can check the alerts?”
    3. Get it on the calendar during the call.
      A calendar invite sent while you’re talking beats a promise to schedule later.
    4. Give them a role.
      Shared actions build commitment on both sides.
      Stronger“I’ll draft the plan; could you send three sites you like?”
    5. Confirm it in writing.
      A short recap email right after the call prevents confusion.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “This sounds good. Let me think about it.”
    You: “Of course. To make that easier, how about I send a one-page summary by tomorrow, and we take 15 minutes Friday to answer any questions? Does 10 a.m. your time work?”Specific, small, scheduled.
    Prospect: “Friday at 10 works.”
    You: “Great, I’ll send the invite now.”Locked in before the call ends.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • “I’ll follow up sometime next week.”
    • A next step that’s too big (“sign the contract”).
    • No written recap.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write the closing line you’ll use to end your next discovery call with a scheduled next step.
    Show an example answer
    “I’ll send a one-page plan by Wednesday. Can we take 15 minutes Thursday at 10 your time to go through it? I’ll send the invite now.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 17Sales conversations
    Questions that make “no” easy
    Asking in a way that’s easy to decline often gets more honest answers.
    
  
    The principle
    Pushing for “yes” makes people defensive. Questions that let them comfortably say “no” lower the pressure and often lead to more honest, more engaged answers.
    
  
    Why it works
    Chris Voss argues that people feel safe and in control when saying “no,” so questions designed to be answered with “no” (like “Would it be a bad idea to…?”) keep conversations open.
    This is a practitioner framework rather than a research finding, so treat it as a technique to test in your own conversations, not a proven rule.
    Framework: Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference (2016). Evidence gap: no controlled research on “no”-oriented questions in sales.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Swap a yes-push for a no-question.
      Let “no” mean agreement.
      Weak“Do you have five minutes?”
      Stronger“Is now a bad time to talk?”
    2. Use it to restart stalled conversations.
      A short no-question in a follow-up email often gets a reply.
      Stronger“Have you given up on fixing the website?”
    3. Use it to check concerns.
      It gives permission to raise objections.
      Stronger“Is there anything in the proposal that doesn’t work for you?”
    4. Keep the tone warm.
      Said coldly, these can sound passive-aggressive.
    5. Respect the answer.
      If they say “yes, I’ve given up,” thank them and let it go.
    
  
    Example in action
    You (follow-up email): “Hi Mark, have you decided to put the website project on hold?”A no-question that’s easy to answer.
    Prospect: “No, not at all! Just been swamped. Can we talk next week?”
    You: “Of course. Would Tuesday at 10 be a bad time?”Keeps the low-pressure tone into the next step.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Using it on every question until it sounds like a script.
    • A sarcastic or guilt-tripping tone.
    • Ignoring a genuine “no.”
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Rewrite these as “no”-oriented questions: (1) “Can we schedule a call?” (2) “Are you still interested?”
    Show an example answer
    1. “Would it be a bad idea to set up a quick call this week?”
2. “Have you given up on improving the website for now?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 18Sales conversations
    Stories over claims
    A short, true client story persuades more than a list of benefits.
    
  
    The principle
    Claims are easy to doubt. Stories are easy to remember. A short, accurate story about a real client, with a problem, what you did, and the result, makes your value concrete.
    
  
    Why it works
    Green and Brock (2000) found that when people were absorbed in a story (“transported”), their beliefs shifted more toward the story’s message and they were less likely to counter-argue.
    Stories only work when they’re true. A made-up or embellished story is deceptive, and it falls apart the moment a prospect asks for a reference.
    Research: Green and Brock, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2000).
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Use a simple structure.
      Problem, what you did, result: three or four sentences.
      Stronger“An appliance store’s web leads went to an inbox no one checked. I connected the form to a text alert. Now the owner sees inquiries within minutes.”
    2. Pick a story that matches their problem.
      Relevance matters more than impressiveness.
    3. Include one vivid, true detail.
      A specific detail makes it memorable and believable.
      Stronger“…he told me he caught a lead while standing in the warehouse.” (only if true)
    4. Be exact about your role and results.
      Say what you did, and only claim results you know.
      Weak“I doubled their sales.”
      Stronger“I set up the alerts. They told me response times improved.”
    5. Keep it short and hand it back.
      Finish by connecting it to them with a question.
      Say“Does something like that happen on your end?”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “I’m not sure a website change would really help us.”
    You: “That’s fair. Can I share a quick example? An appliance store had the same setup: leads came through the form but went to an inbox nobody watched. I connected the form to a text alert, so now the owner sees every inquiry within minutes. Does anything like that happen on your end?”Short, true, relevant, and handed back.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Long stories with no point.
    • Exaggerating your role or the results.
    • Stories from a very different kind of client.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write a three-sentence story from your real work: problem, what you did, result. Only include what’s true.
    Show an example answer
    “A client’s inquiries were getting lost in a buried contact page. I moved the form up, simplified it, and set up alerts to the owner’s phone. Now every request reaches them right away.”
    

    


    
  
    Lesson 19Objections
    “It’s too expensive.”
    Find out what it’s being compared to before you defend the price.
    
  
    The principle
    “Too expensive” almost always means “too expensive compared to something”: another quote, their budget, or the value they see. Don’t defend or discount yet. First find out which one, then respond to that. This is the pattern for every objection: pause, label, clarify, respond, check.
    
  
    Why it works
    Anchoring research (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974) shows a price is judged against a reference point. If you don’t know their reference, you’re answering the wrong question.
    Discounting immediately signals your price was padded and trains clients to push back. Changing scope keeps your price honest.
    Research: Tversky and Kahneman (1974); Gourville (1998). Framework: clarify the objection before answering, common across sales methods. See Lessons 2 and 7.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Pause and label.
      Show you heard it without getting defensive.
      Stronger“It sounds like the price is higher than you expected.”
    2. Clarify the comparison.
      Ask what it’s being compared to.
      Say“Compared to what you had in mind, or to another quote?”
    3. If it’s another quote, compare scope.
      Line by line, honestly.
      Stronger“Let’s look at what each includes. The difference is usually in strategy, copy, or tracking.”
    4. If it’s budget, change scope, not price.
      Offer a smaller option rather than a discount.
      Stronger“With that budget, I’d start with the top three fixes and do the rest later.”
    5. If it’s value, go back to their numbers.
      Reconnect the price to what they told you the problem costs.
      Stronger“You mentioned a client is worth about $2,000. Does it change things that this pays for itself with two?”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Honestly, that’s more than I expected.”
    You: “That’s fair. Can I ask what you were expecting, or is it compared to another quote?”Label and clarify.
    Prospect: “I was thinking closer to $1,500.”
    You: “Thanks for telling me. For $1,500, I’d focus on the three things costing you the most inquiries: the form, the phone number, and the headline. We can do the rest later, if those work. Would that be useful?”Scope changes; price stays honest.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Discounting immediately.
    • Defending the price before you understand the objection.
    • Criticizing the cheaper quote.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your response to “That’s too expensive” for each case: (1) another quote, (2) a limited budget.
    Show an example answer
    1. “That’s a fair comparison to make. Can we look at what each quote includes, side by side?”
2. “Thanks for being upfront. For that budget, I’d start with the fixes that matter most and leave the rest for later.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 20Objections
    “We’ll think about it.”
    Usually a polite way of hiding a concern. Gently find it.
    
  
    The principle
    “We’ll think about it” rarely means they need time to think. It usually means there’s an unspoken concern, or no clear next step. Respect it, gently uncover what’s behind it, and agree on a specific next step.
    
  
    Why it works
    Labeling research (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007) suggests naming the hesitation calmly lowers its intensity and invites people to share what’s really holding them back.
    Implementation intention research (Gollwitzer, 1999; Milkman and colleagues, 2011) shows specific, scheduled plans are far more likely to happen than vague intentions like “we’ll think about it.”
    Research: Lieberman et al. (2007); Gollwitzer (1999); Milkman et al. (2011). See Lessons 14 and 16.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Agree first.
      Never argue with someone wanting time.
      Stronger“Of course, it’s a real decision.”
    2. Ask what they’ll be weighing.
      This often surfaces the real concern.
      Say“So I can help, what’s the main thing you’ll be weighing?”
    3. Label likely concerns.
      If they hesitate, offer a gentle guess.
      Stronger“It sounds like the budget might be the main question.”
    4. Offer something that helps them decide.
      A summary, references, or a smaller first step.
      Stronger“Would a one-page summary with the options help?”
    5. Schedule the next conversation.
      Specific day and time, before you hang up.
      Stronger“Can we take 10 minutes Thursday to answer any questions?”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Let us think about it and get back to you.”
    You: “Of course. So I can make that easier, what’s the main thing you’ll be weighing?”Agree, then clarify.
    Prospect: “I guess whether it’s worth it right now.”
    You: “That makes sense. I’ll send a short summary with what it costs and what it should fix, so you can compare. Could we take 10 minutes Thursday to go over any questions?”Help them decide, then schedule.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Accepting it and never following up.
    • Pushing for a decision on the spot.
    • Following up with “just checking in” and nothing new.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your full response to “We’ll think about it,” ending with a scheduled next step.
    Show an example answer
    “Of course. What’s the main thing you’ll be weighing? … Would a one-page summary help? Let’s take 10 minutes Thursday at 10 to go over it. I’ll send the invite now.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 21Objections
    “We already have someone.”
    Don’t compete with their current person. Find where you fit alongside them.
    
  
    The principle
    Trying to replace someone they already trust rarely works and makes you look pushy. Respect the relationship, find out where the gaps are, and position yourself as a complement: extra capacity, deeper technical help, or a second opinion.
    
  
    Why it works
    Dunford’s positioning framework treats the current solution as the main competitive alternative. You win by being different from it, not by attacking it.
    O’Keefe’s meta-analysis (1999) found that acknowledging the other side and then answering it persuades better than one-sided arguments. Respecting their current provider is part of that.
    Framework: Dunford (2019). Research: O’Keefe (1999). See Lesson 9.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Respect the relationship.
      Agreement lowers defenses.
      Stronger“That’s great; it’s hard to find someone reliable.”
    2. Ask where they’re stretched.
      Even good providers have limits: time, technical depth, or speed.
      Say“What kind of work tends to wait longest?”
    3. Offer to complement, not replace.
      Position yourself as support for the gaps.
      Stronger“Some teams use me for the technical work their designer doesn’t want to handle, or when they need extra hands.”
    4. Leave the door open.
      No pressure, a clear way to reach you.
      Stronger“If anything gets stuck, I’m happy to help, even for a small fix.”
    5. Never criticize their current person.
      It makes you look insecure and puts them on the defensive.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “We already have a web guy.”
    You: “That’s great. Is there anything that tends to wait a while with him, maybe the more technical stuff?”Respect, then a gap question.
    Prospect: “Actually, yes. He doesn’t really do custom functionality.”
    You: “That’s exactly the kind of thing I help with. I could take those on and leave the rest with him.”Complement, not replace.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Trying to replace someone they like.
    • Criticizing their current provider.
    • Ending the conversation without finding a gap.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write two gap questions and one “complement” line for when a prospect already has someone.
    Show an example answer
    Gap: “What kind of work tends to wait longest?” / “Is there anything they’d rather not handle?”
Complement: “I often work alongside designers on the technical side, so they can focus on what they do best.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 22Objections
    “Can you guarantee results?”
    Be honest about what no one can promise, then guarantee what you can.
    
  
    The principle
    No honest provider can guarantee rankings, leads, or revenue, because they depend on things outside anyone’s control. Saying so builds credibility. Then guarantee what you do control: the work, the timeline, and the measurement.
    
  
    Why it works
    The FTC expects claims to have a reasonable basis and guarantees to be honored as advertised, so promising results you can’t control creates legal and reputational risk.
    NN/g’s trust research found people value providers who are upfront about guarantees. A clear, honest guarantee on the process is more credible than a big promise.
    Regulation: FTC advertising substantiation; FTC guides on warranties and guarantees. Research: NN/g (2016). See Lesson 4.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Answer honestly, first.
      Lead with the truth.
      Stronger“Honestly, no one can guarantee a number of leads, and I’d be careful with anyone who does.”
    2. Explain why briefly.
      Results depend on their market, their offer, and their follow-up.
    3. Guarantee what you control.
      Timeline, revisions, communication, and quality.
      Stronger“I guarantee the work: first draft in 7 days and revisions until it matches the plan.”
    4. Offer measurement instead of promises.
      Tracking lets them see results for themselves.
      Stronger“We’ll track inquiries from day one, so you’ll know exactly what changes.”
    5. Reduce risk with structure.
      A small start or milestones replace the need for a result guarantee.
      Stronger“We can start with a small block of hours and decide from there.”
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Can you guarantee more leads?”
    You: “Honestly, no, and no one can, because it depends on things like your market and how fast you respond. What I can guarantee is the work and the measurement.”Honest first.
    You: “You’ll get a first draft in 7 days, revisions until it’s right, and tracking from day one so you see exactly what changes. And you can start small.”Controllable guarantees and low risk.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Promising numbers to win the deal.
    • Answering vaguely, which sounds like dodging.
    • Guarantees with hidden conditions.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your full answer to “Can you guarantee results?”
    Show an example answer
    “Honestly, no one can guarantee leads, because they depend on your market and follow-up. What I guarantee is the work: a first draft in 7 days, revisions until it matches the plan, and tracking from day one so you can see the results yourself.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 23Objections
    “Just send me a proposal.”
    Often a polite exit. Earn the proposal, then walk them through it.
    
  
    The principle
    “Send me a proposal” can be genuine interest or a polite way to end the call. Either way, a proposal sent without a conversation tends to be judged on price alone. Ask a few questions so it fits them, then schedule time to go through it together.
    
  
    Why it works
    Rackham’s SPIN research found that understanding the buyer’s problem and its consequences before proposing was key to larger sales.
    Implementation intention research shows a scheduled walkthrough is far more likely to happen than “I’ll look at it and get back to you.”
    Research: Rackham, SPIN Selling (1988); Gollwitzer (1999). See Lessons 13 and 16.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Agree, then ask permission for questions.
      A good proposal needs answers.
      Stronger“Happy to. So it’s useful and not generic, can I ask three quick questions?”
    2. Ask about decision and budget.
      Who decides, and what range fits.
      Say“Who else will look at it?”
      Say“Is there a budget range you’d like me to stay within?”
    3. Schedule the walkthrough now.
      Proposals read alone get skimmed for the price.
      Stronger“I’ll send it Thursday. Can we take 15 minutes Friday to go through it together?”
    4. Keep the proposal short.
      One or two pages: their goals, the plan, options, price, next step.
    5. Follow Lesson 7’s order.
      Goals and cost of the problem before the price.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “Just send me a proposal.”
    You: “Happy to. So it’s actually useful, can I ask who else will review it, and whether there’s a budget range I should stay within?”Earn the proposal.
    Prospect: “My partner, and ideally under $3,000.”
    You: “Got it. I’ll send it Thursday with a couple of options under that. Could we take 15 minutes Friday with your partner to go through it?”Scheduled walkthrough with the decision maker.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Sending a long proposal with no conversation.
    • Leading with price.
    • No scheduled walkthrough.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your response to “Just send me a proposal,” including two questions and a scheduled walkthrough.
    Show an example answer
    “Happy to. Who else will look at it, and is there a range I should stay within? … I’ll send it Wednesday. Can we take 15 minutes Thursday to go through it together?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 24Objections
    “We tried this before and it didn’t work.”
    Understand what failed before promising it’ll be different.
    
  
    The principle
    A past failure is real and the disappointment is valid. Don’t brush it off or claim you’re different. Find out what went wrong, then show specifically how your approach addresses that cause, and how you’ll measure it this time.
    
  
    Why it works
    Labeling the disappointment (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007) helps lower defensiveness.
    O’Keefe’s meta-analysis found acknowledging a concern and then answering it persuades better than ignoring it. “I’m different” without specifics is ignoring it.
    Research: Lieberman et al. (2007); O’Keefe (1999). See Lessons 11 and 14.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Label the frustration.
      Acknowledge it before anything else.
      Stronger“It sounds like you spent money and didn’t see much come back.”
    2. Diagnose what failed.
      Ask what was done and what happened.
      Say“What did they change, and how did you measure it?”
    3. Address the specific cause.
      Tie your approach to what went wrong.
      Stronger“It sounds like nobody tracked results, so you couldn’t tell what worked. I’d set that up first.”
    4. Offer measurement and a small start.
      Let them see progress before committing more.
      Stronger“Let’s start with one fix and track it for a month.”
    5. Don’t blame the previous provider.
      Focus on what was missing, not who failed.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “We paid someone to redo the site two years ago. Nothing changed.”
    You: “That sounds frustrating, paying for something that didn’t move the needle. What did they change, and how did you track it?”Label, then diagnose.
    Prospect: “New design mostly. We didn’t really track anything.”
    You: “That’s common. A new look doesn’t always change how people decide to call. This time, I’d set up tracking first, then fix the parts that affect inquiries, so you can see exactly what changes.”Specific cause, specific answer, measurement.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • “That won’t happen with me.”
    • Blaming the previous provider.
    • Promising different results without explaining why.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write two diagnostic questions and one response that addresses a likely cause of the past failure.
    Show an example answer
    Questions: “What did they change?” / “How did you measure whether it worked?”
Response: “Since nothing was tracked, the first step would be measurement, so we can see what actually changes.”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 25Objections
    “We’re too busy right now.”
    Show how little of their time it takes, or respect that now isn’t the time.
    
  
    The principle
    “Too busy” is often true, and it’s also often a sign they expect the project to eat their time. Show how little they’ll actually need to do, connect it to the cost of waiting, and if it’s genuinely not the right time, schedule a check-in instead of pushing.
    
  
    Why it works
    Loss framing (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981) helps make the cost of delay visible, while implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999) make a future start more likely when it’s specific.
    Many busy owners avoid projects because they fear the time cost, so showing a low-effort process removes the real barrier.
    Research: Tversky and Kahneman (1981); Gollwitzer (1999). See Lessons 1 and 16.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Agree it’s a busy time.
      Respect their reality.
      Stronger“That makes sense; it sounds like you’ve got a lot going on.”
    2. Show how little time it takes.
      Be specific about their involvement.
      Stronger“From you, it’s about an hour at the start and 15 minutes a week for feedback. I handle the rest.”
    3. Connect it to the cost of waiting, gently.
      Only if the cost is real.
      Stronger“The busy season is also when a missed inquiry costs the most.”
    4. If it’s truly not the time, schedule a check-in.
      A specific date beats “reach out later.”
      Stronger“Would it be a bad idea to check in the first week of March?”
    5. Don’t push.
      Pressure on a busy person ends the relationship.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “We’re slammed right now. Maybe later.”
    You: “Totally understand. Is it the time it would take from you?”Agree, then clarify.
    Prospect: “Yeah, I don’t have time to manage a project.”
    You: “That’s fair. From you, it’d be about an hour to start and 15 minutes a week. If that’s still too much now, would early March be a bad time to check in?”Low effort, or a scheduled later start.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Pushing someone who’s genuinely overwhelmed.
    • Vague “I’ll reach out later.”
    • Hiding how much time the project needs from them.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write how much of the client’s time your main offer actually needs, in one sentence, then a check-in line.
    Show an example answer
    “From you, it’s about an hour at the start and 15 minutes a week for feedback.”
“Would it be a bad idea to check in the first week of March?”
    

    

    
  
    Lesson 26Objections
    “You don’t have experience in our industry.”
    Agree honestly, show what transfers, and offer a fair trade.
    
  
    The principle
    If it’s true, say so. Then show what does transfer (similar problems, similar customers, the same mechanics) and offer something that lowers their risk, like measurement or a lower first-project price.
    
  
    Why it works
    O’Keefe’s meta-analysis (1999) found that two-sided messages that acknowledge a weakness and then answer it are more persuasive than one-sided ones, while acknowledging without answering is less persuasive.
    Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius (2008) found proof from similar people is most persuasive, so show the closest real match you have, honestly labeled.
    Research: O’Keefe (1999); Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius (2008). See Lessons 11 and 12.
    
  
    Guidelines
    1. Agree honestly.
      Don’t stretch adjacent work into industry experience.
      Stronger“You’re right, I haven’t worked with a company in your industry yet.”
    2. Show what transfers.
      Same customer behavior, same mechanics, similar businesses.
      Stronger“I’ve built lead-focused sites for home services companies; your customers decide in a similar way.”
    3. Turn it into a learning advantage.
      A fresh view can spot what insiders miss, if you ask good questions.
      Stronger“I’ll ask a lot of questions about your customers, which often surfaces things insiders overlook.”
    4. Offer a fair trade.
      Lower risk in exchange for being an early client.
      Stronger“Because you’d be one of my first in your industry, I’d reduce the rate and set up tracking so we both see the results.”
    5. Never fake it.
      Don’t claim industry knowledge you don’t have.
    
  
    Example in action
    Prospect: “You haven’t worked with dental practices, have you?”
    You: “Not yet, and I want to be upfront about that. What I have done is build sites for local service businesses where people decide based on trust and convenience, which is very similar for patients.”Honest, then what transfers.
    You: “Since you’d be my first practice, I’d set up tracking before changing anything and adjust the rate. That way, the risk is on me, not you.”A fair trade.
    
  
    Common mistakes
    • Claiming experience you don’t have.
    • Confessing the gap without answering it.
    • Over-apologizing.
    
  
    Try it
    
  
    Write your response for an industry you haven’t worked in: honest admission, what transfers, and a fair trade.
    Show an example answer
    “You’re right, I haven’t worked in your industry yet. I’ve built lead-focused sites for local service businesses, and your customers decide in a similar way. Since you’d be one of my first, I’d set up tracking first and lower the rate.”
